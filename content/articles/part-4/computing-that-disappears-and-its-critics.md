## Abstract

Ubiquitous computing is the vision of computers embedded throughout everyday environments so that they recede from attention. It set much of HCI’s research agenda for mobile and embedded systems, and later critics argue for engagement instead of calm and for studying the messy ubiquitous computing that already exists.

> **From the reading list:** Weiser's vision of invisible, calm computing set the agenda; later work argues the agenda should be engagement, and that ubicomp already exists in messy local forms.

## History

- 1991 — Weiser’s Scientific American article describes tabs, pads and boards working together invisibly.
- 1996 — Weiser and Brown describe calm technology that moves between the periphery and center of attention.
- 2006 — Rogers proposes moving from calm computing to engaging UbiComp experiences.
- 2007–2011 — Bell and Dourish argue ubicomp already exists in local, messy forms and examine its myths.

## Core concepts

- **Invisible computing** — The goal that technology becomes so familiar it is no longer noticed in use.
- **Calm technology** — Technology that informs without demanding focus, using peripheral attention.
- **Periphery** — What people are aware of without attending to it directly.
- **Seamful design** — Design that exposes system limits and seams rather than hiding them.
- **Proximate future** — Bell and Dourish’s term for a future always described as just around the corner.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Invisibility versus seams: Chalmers and MacColl argued for “seamful design” that exposes system limits. Calm versus engagement (Rogers), and privacy and surveillance critiques (Langheinrich, 2001) challenge the vision.
- **Responses and new developments.** Seamful design, privacy-by-design principles for ubicomp and Bell and Dourish's turn to studying actual infrastructure. IoT security research now addresses risks the vision ignored.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Weiser's vision led to the Internet of Things, smart homes, sensor networks and mobile computing, and it founded the UbiComp community.

## Discussion questions

- Is today’s smartphone a fulfillment of Weiser’s vision or a contradiction of it?
- Rogers argues for engagement rather than calm. When is each the right goal?
- Bell and Dourish point to Singapore and Korea as examples of existing ubicomp. What do these cases reveal that lab prototypes miss?
