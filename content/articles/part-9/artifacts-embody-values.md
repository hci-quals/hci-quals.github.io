## Abstract

The idea that artifacts embody values holds that technologies are not neutral tools but carry and enforce social and political arrangements. For HCI, this means design choices are also value choices, and methods such as Value Sensitive Design aim to account for those values systematically rather than leaving them implicit.

> **From the reading list:** Technologies can enforce social arrangements, and designers can account for values systematically. Infrastructure studies show how values hide in standards and systems.

## History

- 1980 — Winner's “Do Artifacts Have Politics?” uses examples like Moses’ low bridges to argue technologies can encode social order.
- 1990s — Friedman and colleagues develop Value Sensitive Design, combining conceptual, empirical and technical investigations.
- 1999 — Star’s ethnography of infrastructure shows values hidden in standards, classifications and mundane systems.
- 2007–2019 — Studies like Sabbath home automation show value-specific design; Friedman and Hendry consolidate VSD methods in a 2019 book.

## Core concepts

- **Politics of artifacts** — The claim that technical designs can settle social questions of power, access and authority.
- **Value Sensitive Design** — A theory and method that accounts for human values throughout the design process.
- **Tripartite methodology** — VSD’s iteration among conceptual, empirical and technical investigations.
- **Direct and indirect stakeholders** — People who use a system and people affected by it without using it.
- **Infrastructural invisibility** — Infrastructure tends to disappear from view until it breaks, hiding the values built into it.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Joerges's “Do Politics Have Artefacts?” (1999) disputed the historical accuracy of Winner's Moses bridges story. VSD was criticized for vague answers to whose values count and for assuming universal values (Le Dantec, Poole and Wyche, CHI 2009).
- **Responses and new developments.** Friedman and Hendry's book adds value tensions and stakeholder methods. Infrastructure studies (Star) show how to find values in standards.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Values-in-design work fed privacy by design, later written into GDPR. It also shaped FAccT and responsible-AI frameworks.

## Discussion questions

- Winner distinguishes designed-in politics from inherently political technologies. Which category fits the systems you build, and does the distinction hold up?
- Can VSD decide whose values count, or does it mainly make value conflicts visible?
- What does Sabbath home automation suggest about designing for values that differ from those of most designers?
