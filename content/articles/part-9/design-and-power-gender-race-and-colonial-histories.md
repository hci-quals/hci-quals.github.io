## Abstract

This idea holds that design can reproduce inequalities of gender, race and colonial history, and that critical theories offer ways to see and change this. For HCI it shifts attention from usability for a generic user toward who benefits, who is harmed, and who gets to design, often favoring processes led by affected communities.

> **From the reading list:** Critical theories show how design reproduces inequality and propose ways to design differently, often led by affected communities.

## History

- 2010 — Bardzell outlines feminist interaction design qualities; Irani et al. propose postcolonial computing as a lens on HCI4D.
- 2016 — Dombrowski, Harmon and Fox lay out strategies and commitments for social justice-oriented interaction design.
- 2019 — Benjamin names the “New Jim Code,” showing how ostensibly neutral technologies reproduce racial hierarchy.
- 2020 — Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al. adapt critical race theory for HCI; Costanza-Chock publishes Design Justice.

## Core concepts

- **Feminist interaction design qualities** — Bardzell’s qualities such as pluralism, participation, advocacy, ecology, embodiment and self-disclosure.
- **Postcolonial computing** — A lens that attends to culture, power and uneven economic relations in design and development.
- **Interest convergence** — The critical race theory claim that racial progress happens mainly when it serves dominant groups’ interests.
- **New Jim Code** — Benjamin’s term for technologies that encode racial discrimination while appearing objective.
- **Design justice** — An approach that centers people marginalized by design and distributes design benefits and burdens fairly.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Should HCI research take advocacy positions? Some critics see feminist and CRT work as political, while its authors argue all design is political. The labor of this work falls disproportionately on scholars of color.
- **Responses and new developments.** Design justice networks, follow-up CRT and feminist HCI work and community-led research models.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** These frames shaped algorithmic fairness research, including Gender Shades (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018) on face recognition, and FAccT's agenda.

## Discussion questions

- Bardzell’s frameworks have been widely cited but less often applied. What would it take to use them in an actual design project?
- Do these frameworks ask designers to design differently, or to step back and let communities lead? Where do they disagree?
- How does storytelling in the critical race theory paper function as evidence, and how should HCI review it?
