## Abstract

The representational effect is the finding that the same information, presented in different forms, can make a task easy or hard. It links cognitive science to visualization and notation design in HCI by explaining why the choice of representation matters as much as its content.

> **From the reading list:** The same information in a different form can make a problem easy or hard. This idea links cognitive science to visualization (Part VIII) and notation (Part VII).

## History

- 1985 — Kotovsky, Hayes and Simon show isomorphs of the Tower of Hanoi differ greatly in difficulty.
- 1987 — Larkin and Simon distinguish informational from computational equivalence of representations.
- 1993 — Norman argues that well-chosen representations make people 'smart' and poor ones mislead.
- 1994 — Zhang and Norman show how splitting a problem's rules between mind and world changes difficulty.

## Core concepts

- **Informational equivalence** — Two representations from which the same information can be inferred.
- **Computational equivalence** — Two representations that also support the same inferences with similar ease and speed.
- **Locality and indexing** — Diagrams group related information by location, reducing search and recognition costs.
- **Internal vs. external representations** — The split between rules held in memory and constraints built into the world, which Zhang and Norman manipulate.
- **Naturalness** — Norman's idea that representations fit better when their properties match the properties of what they represent.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Scaife and Rogers (1996) argued claims about graphical representations outran the evidence and ignored how people actually use them. Benefits depend on expertise and on fit between representation and task.
- **Responses and new developments.** Cognitive fit theory (Vessey, 1991) and Ainsworth's framework for multiple representations (2006) specify when representations help.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** These ideas underpin visualization and visual languages. In AI, the choice of representation shaping search goes back to Amarel (1968) and informs DSL design in program synthesis.

## Discussion questions

- Larkin and Simon say 'sometimes.' Using their account, when would a diagram be worse than text?
- Scaife and Rogers argued these claims outran the evidence. What evidence would convince you that a visualization helps more than a table?
- How would Zhang and Norman analyze a chat interface, where most constraints live in the user's head rather than on screen?
