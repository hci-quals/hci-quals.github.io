## Abstract

Distributed cognition is the view that cognitive processes can span people, artifacts and environments, so that the unit of analysis may be a whole system rather than an individual mind. For HCI it reframes interfaces as parts of cognitive systems and raises current questions about when offloading thinking to tools helps and when it erodes skill.

> **From the reading list:** The unit of analysis can be a system of people and artifacts, and people actively use the world to think. Cognitive offloading research now measures when this helps and when it erodes skill, a live question for AI tools.

## History

- 1994 — Kirsh and Maglio show Tetris players rotate pieces to think, not just to place them.
- 1995 — Hutchins analyzes how a cockpit, not a single pilot, remembers landing speeds.
- 1998 — Clark and Chalmers argue that a notebook can be part of a person's mind.
- 2000–2016 — Hollan, Hutchins and Kirsh propose distributed cognition for HCI; Risko and Gilbert review cognitive offloading.

## Core concepts

- **Distributed cognition** — Analyzing cognition as the propagation of representations across people, artifacts and time.
- **Epistemic action** — Physical action taken to make mental computation easier, rather than to reach a goal state directly.
- **Pragmatic action** — Action that brings a person physically closer to their goal.
- **Extended mind** — The claim that external resources can be literal parts of a cognitive process when suitably coupled.
- **Cognitive offloading** — Using physical action or external tools to reduce the cognitive demands of a task.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Adams and Aizawa charge extended mind with confusing causal coupling with constitution. The “Google effect” on memory (Sparrow et al., 2011) has had mixed replications, and whether offloading frees or erodes memory is unsettled.
- **Responses and new developments.** HCI mostly uses distributed cognition as a method, not a metaphysical claim. Offloading studies now identify conditions, such as Storm and Stone (2015) finding that saving a file helps people learn the next one.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Distributed cognition shapes CSCW, aviation and healthcare safety analysis. Offloading research now anchors debates about AI and deskilling (Human-AI, Key idea 3).

## Discussion questions

- Hutchins describes a cognitive system; Clark and Chalmers make a claim about minds. Does HCI need the stronger claim, or only the method?
- When does offloading to a tool free attention for harder work, and when does it erode a skill? Design a study to tell the two apart for an AI coding assistant.
- What would an epistemic-action analysis of a modern interface (a spreadsheet, a chat assistant) reveal that a task analysis misses?
