## Abstract

Self-presentation and governance online concerns how people manage identity before audiences they cannot see, and how communities set and enforce norms, often through volunteer moderators. It matters for HCI because platform design shapes both who sees what people share and who does the work of keeping spaces usable.

> **From the reading list:** People manage identity before imagined audiences, and communities govern themselves through volunteer labor that platforms depend on.

## History

- 1959 — Goffman describes social life as performance with front and back stages.
- 2011 — Marwick and boyd describe context collapse and the imagined audience on Twitter.
- 2018 — Gillespie argues content moderation is central to what platforms do.
- 2019 — Matias describes Reddit’s volunteer moderators as doing civic labor.

## Core concepts

- **Front and back stage** — Goffman’s distinction between performances for an audience and private settings where people prepare.
- **Context collapse** — When many distinct audiences see the same content, making audience-specific self-presentation hard.
- **Imagined audience** — The audience a person pictures when posting, which may differ from the real one.
- **Civic labor** — Unpaid work that sustains a community’s governance and shared life.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Real names versus pseudonymity, unpaid moderator labor and who should govern, platforms or communities. Commercial moderators face trauma (Roberts, Behind the Screen, 2019).
- **Responses and new developments.** Studies of human-machine moderation such as Reddit's AutoModerator (Jhaver et al., TOCHI 2019) and community governance tools.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Moderation research informs ML toxicity classifiers like Perspective API. It also shapes governance on decentralized social networks such as Mastodon.

## Discussion questions

- Goffman wrote about face-to-face interaction. Which of his concepts break down online and which still fit?
- Platforms depend on volunteer moderators. What do platforms owe them, and what power should communities keep?
- Does pseudonymity reduce context collapse, or does it make governance harder?
