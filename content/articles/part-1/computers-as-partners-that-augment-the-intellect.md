## Abstract

Intellect augmentation is the view that computers should extend human thinking and problem solving rather than replace it. It gave HCI one of its founding goals and frames today's debates over whether AI should act as a copilot or an autopilot.

> **From the reading list:** The earliest visions cast the computer as a partner that amplifies human intellect rather than replacing it. These texts are the ancestors of today's arguments about AI as copilot versus autopilot.

## History

- 1945 — Vannevar Bush describes the memex, a desk for storing and linking a person's records by associative trails.
- 1960 — J.C.R. Licklider proposes man-computer symbiosis, with people setting goals and machines doing routine work.
- 1962 — Engelbart's framework treats humans, tools, language and training as one system to be augmented together.
- 2000s–2010s — Bardini's history and Victor's talks revisit augmentation after personal computing favored ease of use.

## Core concepts

- **Memex** — Bush's imagined desk device for storing documents and linking them by associative trails.
- **Man-computer symbiosis** — Licklider's picture of tight, real-time cooperation in which people and computers each do what they do best.
- **H-LAM/T system** — Engelbart's term for a Human using Language, Artifacts and Methodology, in which he is Trained.
- **Bootstrapping** — Engelbart's strategy of using the tools a group builds to improve its own work, so the group co-evolves with its tools.
- **Augmentation vs. automation** — The split between amplifying human reasoning (IA) and replacing it (AI).

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Augmentation (IA) versus automation (AI) split early computing: McCarthy and Minsky aimed to replace human reasoning, Engelbart to amplify it. Engelbart's chord keyset and long training also clashed with the later ideal of walk-up usability.
- **Responses and new developments.** Personal computing chose ease of use, which sidelined training-heavy augmentation (Bardini). Tools-for-thought work revives the goal but rarely measures long-term skill gains, and Shneiderman's HCAI argues automation and control can coexist.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Bush is cited by Nelson and Berners-Lee as an inspiration for hypertext and the web. Licklider's ARPA office funded time-sharing, graphics and networking, seeding many CS departments; today's “copilot” framing descends directly from symbiosis.

## Discussion questions

- Engelbart accepted long training as the price of augmentation. Was personal computing's focus on ease of use a loss, and what would we measure to find out?
- Which parts of Licklider's division of labor between people and machines still hold for large language models, and which have flipped?
- Victor argues current representations use a narrow slice of human ability. Is that a critique of tools or of knowledge work itself?
