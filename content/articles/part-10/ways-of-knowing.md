## Abstract

Ways of knowing refers to the distinct ways HCI produces knowledge, including technical building, ethnography and action research, each with its own standards of rigor. Recognizing them matters because work judged by the wrong standard is easily dismissed, and because HCI depends on combining these traditions.

> **From the reading list:** HCI produces knowledge through technical building, ethnography and action research, each with its own standards.

## History

- 1990s — Ethnography enters CSCW and HCI alongside experimental and engineering traditions.
- 2000s — Debates over evaluating systems, ethnography and design research highlight differing standards.
- 2014 — Olson and Kellogg publish Ways of Knowing in HCI, with chapters on technical research (Hudson and Mankoff), ethnography (Dourish) and action research (Hayes).

## Core concepts

- **Technical HCI research** — Knowledge produced by inventing and building systems that make new interactions possible.
- **Ethnography** — Long-term immersive study aimed at understanding practices and meanings in their own terms.
- **Action research** — Research conducted with communities to bring about change they value.
- **Epistemic standards** — The criteria a tradition uses to judge whether knowledge is valid or useful.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Pluralism can become incommensurability, with technical and qualitative researchers talking past each other.
- **Responses and new developments.** Mixed-methods research and edited volumes like Ways of Knowing aim for mutual literacy.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** The volume is used mainly in HCI methods training.

## Discussion questions

- What counts as rigor for a technical paper compared with an ethnography or an action research project?
- Can a single paper satisfy reviewers from more than one way of knowing?
- Hayes describes action research as shared with communities. How does that change authorship and evaluation?
