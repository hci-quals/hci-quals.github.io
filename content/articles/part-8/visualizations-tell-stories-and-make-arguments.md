## Abstract

This idea holds that visualizations communicate and persuade, not just display data, through narrative structure, framing and animation. It matters for HCI because design choices can guide or mislead viewers, raising questions about rhetoric and responsibility.

> **From the reading list:** No chart is neutral: design choices frame data, and narrative and animation can help or mislead.

## History

- 2002 — Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt find little evidence that animation beats static graphics when content is equal.
- 2007 — Heer and Robertson show well-designed animated transitions help viewers track changes in charts.
- 2010–2011 — Segel and Heer define narrative visualization genres; Hullman and Diakopoulos analyze framing as rhetoric.
- 2014 — Nielsen's 'Reinventing Explanation' argues new media can create new forms of explanation.

## Core concepts

- **Narrative visualization** — Visualizations designed to convey a story, from author-driven to reader-driven.
- **Author-driven vs. reader-driven** — How much the designer controls the sequence versus letting viewers explore.
- **Visualization rhetoric** — Design techniques that frame data and influence how viewers interpret it.
- **Congruence and apprehension** — Tversky et al.'s principles that animation should match the concept and be easy to perceive.
- **Animated transition** — Animation between two views of data that helps viewers keep track of what changed.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Persuasive visualization raises ethics questions; Correll et al. (CHI 2020) found truncated y-axes exaggerate differences even when readers are warned. Animation can increase engagement while hurting accuracy.
- **Responses and new developments.** Studies of deceptive visualizations (Pandey et al., CHI 2015) and guidelines for when transitions help.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** This work shaped data journalism and scrollytelling at outlets like the New York Times. It also informs presentation software.

## Discussion questions

- Tversky et al. are skeptical of animation, while Heer and Robertson find benefits. How can both be right?
- Is all visualization rhetorical? Where is the line between framing and manipulation?
- What would count as a genuinely new form of explanation in Nielsen's sense?
