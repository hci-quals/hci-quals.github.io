## Abstract

Learning by building, or constructionism, is the view that people learn best when they are actively making shareable artifacts, such as programs, games or stories. It has shaped HCI work on educational programming environments, from Logo to Scratch, and on designing tools around creative expression rather than drill.

> **From the reading list:** People learn powerful ideas by constructing things they care about. Scratch carried this from Logo to millions of children.

## History

- 1967 — Papert, Feurzeig and colleagues create Logo and the turtle as an object to think with.
- 1980 — Mindstorms argues that computers can let children learn mathematics the way they learn language.
- 2007 — The MIT Media Lab launches Scratch, a block-based language with an online sharing community.
- 2009 — Resnick et al. describe Scratch's design goals: low floor, wide walls, high ceiling, and remixing.

## Core concepts

- **Constructionism** — Learning happens especially well when people construct meaningful, shareable artifacts.
- **Object to think with** — A concrete artifact, like the Logo turtle, that connects bodily knowledge to abstract ideas.
- **Low floor, wide walls** — Easy to start, and supportive of many different kinds of projects.
- **Remixing** — Building on and modifying others' shared projects as a path into programming.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argue minimally guided discovery learning does not work. Early Logo studies found little transfer of programming to general reasoning (Pea and Kurland, 1984).
- **Responses and new developments.** Guided inquiry, structured Scratch curricula and the computational thinking agenda (Wing, 2006) try to keep construction while adding guidance.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Constructionism shaped CS education policy, block-based languages (Scratch, Blockly, App Inventor) and educational robotics.

## Discussion questions

- Papert made strong claims about transforming education. What evidence would support or refute them, and has it appeared?
- How does Scratch's 'wide walls' extend the threshold–ceiling framing from the toolkit literature?
- Is the online community, rather than the language, the main reason Scratch succeeded?
