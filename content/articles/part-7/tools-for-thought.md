## Abstract

Tools for thought are media and software meant to expand human thinking and understanding, not just to complete tasks. The idea links HCI to a long tradition from Bush and Engelbart, and recent essays debate whether such tools should be judged as artifacts or as cultural practices.

> **From the reading list:** Tools can change how people think, but the change lives in practices, not apps. Learning science explains why some practices, like retrieval, actually work.

## History

- 1945–1960s — Bush's memex and Engelbart's augmenting human intellect frame computers as aids to thought.
- 2006 — Roediger and Karpicke show retrieval practice improves long-term retention, a result later built into spaced-repetition tools.
- 2011–2012 — Victor's essays argue programming environments should make behavior visible and explorable.
- 2019–2022 — Matuschak and Nielsen propose transformative tools for thought; Appleton reframes them as cultural practices.

## Core concepts

- **Augmentation** — Using technology to extend human intellect rather than to replace it.
- **Mnemonic medium** — A text with embedded spaced-repetition prompts so that readers remember what they read.
- **Testing effect** — Retrieving information from memory strengthens retention more than restudying.
- **Live, visible programming** — Environments that show the program's state and behavior as it is edited.
- **Practice-based view** — The idea that tools shape thought only through the communities and habits that use them.

## Questions, controversies, and new developments

- **Controversies.** Enthusiasm outpaces evidence: note-taking apps are rarely evaluated, and spaced repetition is proven for facts but less so for conceptual understanding. Matuschak and Nielsen note the work fits poorly in academia or industry.
- **Responses and new developments.** Matuschak's mnemonic-medium experiments and learning-science findings on desirable difficulties (Bjork) supply some evidence.

## Broader connections

- **Influence.** Retrieval-practice research underpins Anki and SuperMemo. The movement shaped apps like Roam and Obsidian and now frames research on AI and thinking.

## Discussion questions

- Many of these readings are essays, not peer-reviewed papers. What counts as evidence for a tool for thought?
- Matuschak and Nielsen ground their work in cognitive science. Does Appleton's cultural-practice view undercut or complement this?
- Which of Victor's learnable-programming principles appear in today's tools, and which never caught on?
