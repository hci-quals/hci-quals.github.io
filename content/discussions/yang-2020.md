## Main idea

It is uniquely difficult to design human–AI interaction for two reasons. First, *uncertainty about the capability of AI*, especially with learning systems that evolve. A benchmark can reduce that uncertainty, but only as far as the benchmark itself is good; Microsoft Tay was used as an example. Second, *complexity of outputs*. Traditional HCI methods for prototyping and evaluation usually assume a fairly fixed set of outcome states. With an adaptive system it may be quite difficult to anticipate everything ahead of time.

## What is the paper trying to do?

Answer a salient question: why is it hard to design systems that have AI in them? The paper offers two primary challenges, and a way of thinking about when those difficulties arise.

## What kind of contribution is it?

A conceptual paper, an instantiation that looks back at the founding visions. The two reasons above are how it meets those visions.

## Why does it matter?

It names a change in what HCI is asked to design. If capabilities are uncertain and outputs are not a fixed set of states, the interaction cannot be designed the way a website with a known set of paths can.

## Hot takes / discussion

It is a meta-application of Licklider. Previously, HCI people had been doing a lot of planning — analogously to programmers writing down the exact instructions a computer should follow, they designed the exact way a user should move through a website. With AI, and further with just-in-time interfaces, the work shifts toward goal or intent: the “program” is replaced by an interface produced on the fly to help people accomplish what they want.

The shift is from designing exact user paths to designing for intent and just-in-time interfaces. A related open question: where does the line sit between symbiotic augmentation and systems that can generate their own software or interfaces?
