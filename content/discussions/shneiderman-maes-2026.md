## Main idea

Maes and Shneiderman return to their 1997 argument for the era of generative AI agents. The classic positions still hold: Shneiderman wants people close to decisions, with a sense of control and justified responsibility; Maes argues that a social, changing world requires agents that can act beyond what the user directly observes, on the basis of personalized preferences. In the reprise, a sharp remaining disagreement is over the *word* “agent.” Shneiderman treats it as more than terminology: agents are not just “super tools,” because the agent metaphor implies delegated responsibility, whereas tools leave responsibility with the user.

## What is the paper trying to do?

Replay and update the 1997 debate as a keynote, testing whether the old opposition still organizes current choices about assistants and agents. The discussion used a transcript of the reprise together with the 1997 text, and spent much of the time asking what, if anything, the two still disagree about.

## What kind of contribution is it?

A staged debate (a reprise, not a new empirical study), used here as an instantiation alongside the 1997 paper, and as a place to test false dichotomies of control and of delegation versus collaboration.

## Why does it matter?

It shows which parts of the old argument have become a disagreement about scope — different tasks, or agents that only work on top of a good interface — and which part is still substantive: whether an agent metaphor changes who is responsible, and how far a person is from the decisions that get made.

## Hot takes / discussion

The reprise was read as heated, including pointed remarks about consistency with earlier positions. Much of it can still be heard as two ships passing in the night, aimed at different ranges of tasks. Maes’s concession that agents work best with a good underlying interface, and that stable domains can be well served by interfaces alone, supports that reading.

The disagreement that does not reduce to scope is responsibility and metaphor. If “agent” implies that responsibility has been handed off, the choice of words is a design stance, not a label. In a more social, web-like environment, though, responsibility is already messier, and a pure assignment of it is harder to maintain.

That concern was hooked back to Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens’s 1–10 scale. At level 6 the human has a restricted time to veto before automatic execution; above that, the system acts and the human is not told. On a coarse reading, levels 1–6 leave responsibility with the human and levels 7–10 do not — with a possible softening at 6, if time pressure makes the veto unreal. Responsibility, on this account, is treated more coarsely than the rest of the framework.

A 2023 paper of Shneiderman’s was brought in as a further wrinkle: user control versus system control is not a zero-sum axis. High *perceived* control for both is possible when the user’s goals and the system’s goals are aligned and the system is trusted to carry those goals out. (That still requires trusting that the system’s goals will stay aligned.) Delegation versus collaboration was framed as another false dichotomy: the two approaches are often pitted against each other, but both direct-manipulation and agent-style designs can support both. An open question left on the table is what a richer account of *distance from design decisions* would look like — the sense that direct manipulation stays close to the choices one makes, and agents recede from them.
