## Main idea

Knowledge workers reported thinking less, and doing less critical thinking, when using AI. The pattern is tied to confidence: higher self-confidence led to more critical thinking; higher confidence in the AI led to more offloading and less thinking.

## What is the paper trying to do?

Survey knowledge workers on generative AI use, cognitive effort, and critical thinking. Under “how does this relate to the idea readings,” the notes reconstruct a sequence that maps onto goal-setting, instruction, and verification: (1) before engaging a tool, people reflect on goals, needs, and intents, and identify a need for assistance; (2) they translate that into computer instructions (for example, a query); (3) they verify, across domains, against both objective criteria for the task and subjective criteria for the individual (internal consistency, and so on), with source references as one other metric; (4) part of verification is also *style* and the integration of the responses. The notes ask why people think less critically — cognitive offloading gone too far? — and observe that how people articulate a query can decline after initial verification.

## What kind of contribution is it?

An empirical survey of knowledge work with generative AI, used in this session as an instantiation of goal formulation and verification.

## Why does it matter?

It shows, in ordinary work, the confidence and offloading effects that accompany AI use, and it raises a problem about verification. The notes call verification a two-edged sword: if people do not know which dimensions to verify, how can they know whether what they are verifying is correct?

## Hot takes / discussion

Two distinctions organize the notes. One is *derivable versus true*: what is logically consistent is not the same as a true or good outcome, which may require value judgment. The other is that goals often cannot be fully specified. The group pointed to Herbert Simon’s “The Structure of Ill-Structured Problems” and recorded a related link (`https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04274`). Uncertainty about goals often leads users to defer granularity to the model, which raises a design question recorded in the Zoom summary: how can systems help people refine their goals and establish better criteria for success, rather than only producing an answer?

The closing of the Zoom transcript asked where modern programs follow this direction, and whether we should probe people’s values to check whether an output program fits, alongside the verifiable part (does the program work?). Qualitative studies were mentioned as a way to get a thicker description, with the further question of how to embed that in real-time interaction.
